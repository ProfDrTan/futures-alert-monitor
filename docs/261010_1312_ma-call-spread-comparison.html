What the pros and cons actually mean

Butterfly, "weak if midstrike reached quickly". A butterfly earns most of its value from time decay in the last day or two before expiry. If MA hits 600 on Monday, the butterfly is still worth only a fraction of its 10-point maximum, because the two short 600 calls still have time value. It pays best when MA drifts to 600 and sits there into Friday.

Reverse mullet, "can be disassembled". This trade is really two spreads stacked together: a 590/600 bull call spread (the vertical) plus a short 600/602.5 call spread. If MA rallies hard, you can buy back the short 600/602.5 piece and be left holding the plain vertical, which pays the full 10-point width above 600. The asymmetric upper wing is what keeps the trade profitable on an overshoot, unlike the normal butterfly.

Vertical, "can't be disassembled". With only two legs there is nothing to peel off. You either hold the spread or close it. It is the simplest of the three and the most expensive.

How to choose. Use the butterfly when you have a precise target and expect a slow drift. Use the reverse mullet when you expect a move to the target but want protection if it overshoots. Use the vertical when you are confident of direction but not of the exact landing point.

4. Caveats

These are payoffs at expiry only. Values during the week depend on implied volatility and time, and were not modelled here. Prices were frozen Friday-close data and will differ at Monday's open. "Reverse mullet" appears to be ShadowTrader's own name for this asymmetric butterfly; it may not be a standard industry term. This page is analysis for study, not a trade recommendation.
