Case Study 2: The Nasdaq Waterfall

Thursday 5 November 2026 · Prof Tan and Claude · all times Singapore (SGT) · prices from IBKR (MNQ/NQ Dec '26) and thinkorswim · Open

The question this case asks: twelve hours after closing a gold position at breakeven, having just named the psychology that kept him in it (loss aversion, the disposition effect, the break-even effect), what happens when the same trader meets a new, faster loss?

The setting

The post-midterm rally had lifted the Dow by 3% on Wednesday. On Thursday the rotation turned on technology: Qualcomm fell about 10–12% on a soft profit outlook, Arm about 7%, and memory names sharply, while fears that a Democratic Congress would tighten rules on data centres hit the whole AI-infrastructure complex. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell more than 5% early; SK Hynix lost about 9% in Seoul. Nasdaq 100 futures were down about 0.5% before the U.S. open, with sellers waiting at 28,447–28,476.

What happened, minute by minute

21:30–22:30 · Waiting, and a feed that fails

Prof wanted to act on Nasdaq; the IBKR connection to Claude dropped at the worst moment, and Claude worked from news reports and Prof's thinkorswim screenshots until it was reconnected. Claude's read: the trend was down; short a bounce into 28,447–28,476; do not chase.

22:30 · The waterfall

At the New York cash open, NQ fell about 495 points in 15 minutes on 280,067 MNQ contracts, roughly seven times any earlier bar of the day, then another 237. It broke the election-night low of 27,611.5. Claude, at 22:58: do not sell here, after a seven-fold volume flush at a known low; wait for a bounce.

About 23:00 · "No time to react. I just bought a long NQ."

Prof bought one full-size NQ (about $20 a point) at about 27,494.75, a counter-trend dip-buy into the flush. Claude immediately asked for an exit to be set: a stop near 27,410, just under the new 27,433 low (about $1,700 of risk), with first targets at 27,611–27,675. No stop was placed.

23:02–23:29 · The freeze

NQ kept falling: 27,433, then 27,084.75. At 23:29 NQ was 27,206.75 and the position was −$5,760; a short in micro S&P futures, held separately, was +$1,149 and softened the blow. In Prof's words, his loss aversion "kicked in again automatically by default", and he "just kind of froze looking at the screen as it dropped further and further". He was also tired.

23:30 · A plan for the night

Prof asked for limit-sell targets to leave overnight. Claude mapped where Fibonacci retracements, broken support and the day's market profile line up (table below) and recommended one target, 27,570, paired in a one-cancels-other order with a stop at 26,990, under the day's low, good-till-cancelled. The alternative stated plainly: close the trade and sleep with nothing at risk.

23:45 · The temptation of a forecast

Prof raised Tom Lee's call that hard-hit chip and AI stocks would rebound most after the midterms, with tech rallying 15–20% into year-end: "If it's gonna happen, then I'm just gonna hold on." Claude's counterpoint: the forecast runs to Christmas, while the position was one full NQ, overnight, ahead of payrolls; a further 10% drop before any rally would cost about $54,000. If the thesis is believed, hold it at a size that survives the path (two or three micro contracts), or with the stop.

The psychology, named

Loss aversion. A loss feels roughly twice as painful as an equal gain feels good, so closing at a loss is avoided even when the evidence says the trade is wrong.

Freeze. Under acute stress, the response is often neither fight nor flight but stillness: watching the price fall without acting. Fatigue makes it more likely.

Anchoring to the entry. The decision becomes "get back to 27,494" rather than "where is this market going".

Authority and confirmation. When a position hurts, a respected forecaster who agrees with it is easy to reach for. Tom Lee's call may still prove right; using it to justify an unhedged, oversized overnight position is the bias.

The disposition effect, again. Case Study 1 ended with winners sold at breakeven and losers held through a $45,600 drawdown; Case Study 2 began with the same reflex the next evening.

What would have changed the night

  • Rule 2, the stop goes in with the entry. The stop Claude asked for at 27,410 would have capped the loss near $1,700; the freeze happened after the entry, when deciding was hardest.
  • Rule 1, size from the stop. At 1% of the account, the right size for a 100-point stop was about six micro contracts, not one full NQ.
  • Rule 3, never add or enter against a waterfall. Wait for a reclaim (here, 27,611) or a bounce into a confluence zone to sell.
  • Rule 8, do not trade tired. The freeze was the body's answer to a decision made too late in the day.
  • A forecast is not a stop. A year-end thesis needs a position sized for the path, not only for the destination.
